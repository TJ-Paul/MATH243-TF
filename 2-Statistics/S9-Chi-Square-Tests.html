S9 · Chi-Square Tests

Why this matters for the term final. The contingency-table χ2 test appeared in both of the latest papers. 2023-24 Q8(a) (15 marks) gave a 3×3 table of job performance against educational attainment (this is exactly the lecture’s Problem 1), and 2022-23 Q8(a) (10 marks) gave a 2×2 table of drug versus typhoid. Goodness of fit appeared as a short note in 2023-24 Q7(a). Both full solutions are in TF 2023-24 Solutions and TF 2022-23 Solutions; this note teaches the method with fresh practice of each type. The general testing steps are in S8 Hypothesis Testing Concepts and z t Tests.

1. The chi-square statistic

All chi-square tests compare observed frequencies O (what we counted) with expected frequencies E (what we would expect if H0​ were true):

Memorize: The chi-square statistic

χ2=∑Ei​(Oi​−Ei​)2​(equivalently χ2=∑Ei​Oi2​​−N) It follows (approximately) a chi-square distribution with ν degrees of freedom. ==If O and E agree closely, χ2 is small and we accept H0​; if they disagree a lot, χ2 is large and we reject H0​.== The test is always right-tailed: reject H0​ if χcal2​≥χα,ν2​.

Why square and divide by E? Squaring stops positive and negative differences cancelling, and dividing by E makes a difference of 10 count more when we expected 15 than when we expected 1000.

Memorize: Conditions for a valid chi-square test

The observations must be independent and counted (frequencies, not percentages or measurements). The total N should be reasonably large (at least 50). Every expected frequency should be at least 5; if some are smaller, merge neighbouring classes and reduce the degrees of freedom accordingly. The sum of the expected frequencies must equal the sum of the observed frequencies.

Useful critical values: χ0.052​ with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 df is 3.84, 5.99, 7.81, 9.49, 11.07, 12.59; χ0.012​ with 1, 2, 3, 4 df is 6.63, 9.21, 11.34, 13.28.

2. Goodness-of-fit test

Memorize: Goodness of fit

A goodness-of-fit test checks whether an observed frequency distribution agrees with a hypothesized theoretical distribution (uniform, a given ratio, binomial, Poisson, normal, …). H0​: the data follow the stated distribution; H1​: they do not. ν=k−1−m where k is the number of classes (after merging small ones) and m is the number of parameters estimated from the data to compute the expected frequencies (e.g. m=1 when λ of a Poisson is estimated by xˉ; m=0 when all probabilities are given).

Fresh practice 1 (is a die fair?). A die is rolled 120 times: faces 1 to 6 appear 22, 17, 20, 26, 18, 17 times. Test at 5% whether the die is fair.

H0​: the die is fair, so each face has probability 1/6 and expected frequency E=120/6=20. H1​: the die is not fair.

3. Contingency table: test of independence (association)

A contingency table is a two-way table of frequencies in which individuals are classified by two qualitative attributes A (with r categories, the rows) and B (with c categories, the columns). The categories of each attribute must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive.

Memorize: Test of independence in an r×c table

H0​: there is no association between A and B (they are independent). H1​: there is an association. Eij​=NRi​×Cj​​=grand total(row total)×(column total)​

χ2=∑i​∑j​Eij​(Oij​−Eij​)2​,ν=(r−1)(c−1) Reject H0​ if χ2≥χα,(r−1)(c−1)2​.

Where the expected frequency formula comes from. If A and B are independent, P(Ai​∩Bj​)=P(Ai​)P(Bj​)=NRi​​⋅NCj​​, so the expected count in cell (i,j) is N⋅NRi​​⋅NCj​​=NRi​Cj​​. Why (r−1)(c−1) degrees of freedom? With all row and column totals fixed, once we fill in (r−1)(c−1) cells, the remaining cells are forced by the totals.

Exam trick: layout

Write the expected frequency in brackets under each observed frequency inside the original table (exactly as in the lecture), then make the (O−E)2/E column table. Check that ∑O=∑E and ∑(O−E)=0. Keep two or three decimal places in each term.

4. The 2×2 table and Yates’ correction

Memorize: 2×2 contingency table shortcut (lecture formula, with Yates' correction)

B1​B2​Total
A1​aba+b
A2​cdc+d
Totala+cb+dN
$$\chi^2 = \frac{N\left(\leftad - bc\right- \frac{N}{2}\right)^2}{(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)} \sim \chi^2_1$$
Without the correction the formula is χ2=(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)N(ad−bc)2​. Critical value at 5% with 1 df: 3.84.

Yates’ correction for continuity. The chi-square distribution is continuous but frequencies are discrete; with only 1 degree of freedom this makes the uncorrected χ2 slightly too large. Yates suggested reducing ∣ad−bc∣ by N/2 (equivalently reducing each ∣O−E∣ by 0.5). It is especially important when some expected frequencies are small (between 5 and 10). The course’s lecture formula includes the correction, so use it, and you may mention the uncorrected value as well.

Common mistake: "effective" questions

A question like “can it be concluded that the drug is effective?” is answered with this test of independence. If H0​ (independence) is rejected, look at the table to decide the direction of the association (e.g. the typhoid rate is much lower in the drug group), and only then say “the drug is effective”. Rejecting independence by itself only shows that there is an association.

Self-check

1. In a 4×3 table, how many degrees of freedom? (4−1)(3−1)=6.

2. Row total 60, column total 50, grand total 200. Expected frequency? 60×50/200=15.

3. A goodness-of-fit test to a Poisson distribution uses 6 classes and estimates λ from the data. df? 6−1−1=4.

4. Why is the chi-square test right-tailed only? Because χ2 is a sum of non-negative terms; only large values indicate disagreement with H0​.

5. What should you do if an expected frequency is 3? Merge that class with a neighbouring class (and reduce the degrees of freedom), or use Yates’ correction in a 2×2 table.
